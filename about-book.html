Synopsis

In the spring of 1999, as war tears through Kosova, a young woman named Ariana Hoxha boards a plane for the United States—alone, terrified, and traveling under someone else's name. Behind her are her parents and grandmother, missing somewhere in the chaos of the conflict. Ahead of her is a country whose language she barely speaks and a life she cannot yet imagine.

At the center of this gripping narrative is the Gashi family, whose sons are scattered by circumstance—some already abroad, some still facing the war at home—and Ariana, whose red ribbon and small red knife become talismans of everything she has lost and everything she is determined to survive.

Drawing on years of interviews with Kosovar refugees—including conversations with Kosova's former president, Fatmir Sejdiu—author Carol Detweiler has woven their real experiences into a historical narrative that presents the emotional truth of what they endured.

Section One

Part One: A Murder of Crows

Set in Kosova through the late 1980s and 1990s, this first half of the novel immerses readers in the mounting crisis before and during the war—the tightening grip of oppression, the fear that settles over daily life, and the desperate choices families face as violence closes in around them. Through the eyes of Ariana Hoxha and the Gashi family, readers witness a homeland transformed: peaceful traditions and close-knit community give way to loss, separation, and flight. It is a section marked by tension and grief, capturing what it truly cost the people of Kosova to survive—and for some to leave.

Section Two

Part Two: As the Crows Fly

Where the first half chronicles crisis, the second follows what comes after: the resilience of refugees rebuilding their lives in a new country. As Ariana and the Gashi family arrive in the United States, the novel shifts toward resettlement, connection, and the slow, hard-won process of healing. Unfamiliar cities become home, and the promise of a college education and opportunity offers a path forward. Without losing sight of what was lost, nor the sacrifices so many others had made to bring them to safety, this section carries an undercurrent of hope—a testament to the determination and strength it takes not just to survive a war, but to build a new life in its wake.

A Note on the Title

The word "Kosovo" comes from a Slavic phrase meaning "field of blackbirds," referring to a 1389 battle site. A "murder of crows"—the ancient term for a flock of crows—has long been a symbol of battlefields and aftermath. Here, it becomes a haunting image for a land that witnessed devastation, while the Red Ribbon represents the hope, resilience, and optimism of a people who kept moving forward anyway.
